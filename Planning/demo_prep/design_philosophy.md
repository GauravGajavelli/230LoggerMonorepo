Goals: 

Make the value immediately obvious. Students are visiting this tool after they've already submitted, so their motivation to engage is lower than during active work. The landing view should surface the most actionable insight first — not raw data. Lead with something like "Your test for remove() started failing after your change at 2:47 PM" rather than presenting a full timeline and asking students to explore. You want to answer "why should I look at this?" within seconds.

Design the timeline for scanning, not just replaying. A replayable timeline is powerful, but students will lose patience if they have to watch it linearly. Let them jump directly to moments where a test's status changed (pass → fail or fail → pass). Visually mark those transition points — they're the moments that matter most for learning. Color-coding or filtering by test status helps a lot here.

Layer the information progressively. You're presenting three things: what happened (timeline), why it happened (explanations), and what to do (suggestions). Don't show all of these at the same depth simultaneously. A good pattern is to start with a summary card for each failing test, let students expand into the explanation, and from there link to the relevant code diff and suggested next steps. This avoids overwhelming students who just want a quick nudge while still supporting those who want to dig deeper.

Reduce friction around code context. When the tool says "this change likely caused the failure," students need to see the actual diff with minimal effort. Inline side-by-side or unified diffs right in the explanation view — don't make them open a separate page or mentally reconstruct what changed. Syntax highlighting and highlighting the specific changed lines (not just the file) matters a lot for readability.

Consider the emotional dimension. Students seeing a wall of failures can feel discouraging, especially after they thought they were done. Frame things constructively — "3 of 8 tests need attention" reads differently than "3 tests failed." Show progress where possible: if they had 5 failures earlier and now have 3, surface that improvement explicitly.

Important Guidelines: 

Color should encode meaning, not decorate. The red/green/blue system in the wireframe isn't aesthetic — it's a pre-attentive visual channel. Students can scan eight test cards and know the situation before reading a single word. That's cognitive load reduction, which is legitimate and valuable. Where color becomes problematic is when you add gradients, brand colors, or themed palettes that don't carry information. Every color in a research tool should answer the question "what does this tell the student?" If the answer is "nothing, it just looks nice," reconsider it.

Animation should signal state changes, not entertain. The most defensible use of animation in your tool is the expand/collapse transition on test cards. Without it, the interface jumps and the student loses spatial context — "where did that content come from?" A short 150ms ease-out answers that question implicitly. Similarly, if a student clicks a timeline event and the corresponding test card scrolls into view, a smooth scroll is orientation, not decoration. What you want to avoid is entrance animations, loading flourishes, or hover effects that exist to make the tool feel polished. In a research context, those create a variable: are students engaging with the feedback or with the interface?

Contrast and typography do the heaviest lifting. The most effective "CSS trick" for a learning tool isn't flashy at all — it's information hierarchy through font weight, size, and spacing. In the wireframe, the section labels ("What happened," "Code change," "Suggested next step") use small uppercase type to create scannable landmarks. The explanation text is set slightly larger and in a readable line length. The code diffs use a monospace font with colored line highlights. None of this is decorative, but it's doing more for usability than any animation would.

Where restraint matters most for your research. Your consent form mentions tracking "whether feedback was opened, features used, and time spent viewing." This means every visual design decision becomes a potential variable. If you add a satisfying animation to "Mark as reviewed," does that increase completion rates because students feel closure, or because pressing the button is rewarding? You can't easily distinguish those in your data. The cleaner your visual language — meaning every element serves comprehension rather than engagement — the more confidently you can attribute behavioral patterns to the feedback content itself.

The practical guideline. Before adding any visual effect, ask: "If I removed this, would the student misunderstand something or lose their place?" If yes, keep it. If the answer is "no, but it would look worse," that's a signal to leave it out in the research version. You can always add polish later once you've established that the feedback content itself is what's driving the learning outcomes you're measuring.
